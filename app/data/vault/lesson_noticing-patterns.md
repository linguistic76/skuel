## From Noticing Breath to Noticing Yourself

If you've practiced breath awareness, you already have the core skill. You learned to notice when your mind wanders and gently return. Self-observation uses the exact same muscle — but now the object isn't your breath. It's your behavior.

You're not trying to fix anything yet. You're not trying to be better. You're just watching — the same way you watched your breath rise and fall.

## What Is a Pattern?

A pattern is anything that happens more than once in a recognizable way. You probably already know some of yours:

- You check your phone when you're bored
- You get defensive when someone gives you feedback
- You procrastinate on tasks that feel overwhelming
- You say yes when you mean no
- You feel anxious on Sunday evenings

These aren't problems to solve (not yet). They're data. Each one tells you something about how you respond to the world. Self-reflection starts with seeing them clearly.

## The Trigger-Feeling-Response Loop

Most patterns follow a simple structure:

**Trigger** → Something happens (an event, a comment, a situation)
**Feeling** → An emotion arises (frustration, anxiety, excitement, boredom)
**Response** → You do something (react, withdraw, distract, engage)

Example: Someone criticizes your work (trigger) → you feel a flush of defensiveness (feeling) → you argue back immediately (response).

The loop happens fast — often before you're conscious of it. Self-observation is the practice of slowing down enough to see the loop while it's running.

## Pause and Name

This is the simplest self-observation practice. When you notice a reaction happening:

1. **Pause.** Don't act on the reaction immediately. Even one second of pause is enough.
2. **Name what you feel.** Silently: "I'm feeling defensive." "I'm feeling anxious." "I'm feeling pulled toward my phone."

That's it. You don't need to do anything about the feeling. Naming it creates a tiny gap between the trigger and the response. In that gap, choice lives.

This is the same labeling technique from mindfulness meditation, applied to real life. Instead of "thinking" on the cushion, it's "defensive" in a conversation. Same skill, different context.

## Practice: One Pattern, One Day

Pick one pattern you already suspect you have. For one day, just notice when it shows up. Don't try to change it. Just notice.

Some starter patterns to watch for:
- **The phone check** — When do you reach for your phone? What just happened before you reached?
- **The agreement** — When do you say yes when you don't really want to?
- **The avoidance** — What tasks do you keep pushing to tomorrow?

At the end of the day, write one sentence: "I noticed that when [trigger], I felt [feeling] and I [response]."

One sentence. One pattern. One day. That's enough to begin.
